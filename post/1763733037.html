Linear Algebra Chapter 5.1 - 5.3 Eigenvalues, Eigenvectors and Diagonalization

Section 5.1 Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

Definition

An eigenvector of an n×nn\times nn×n matrix AAA is a nonzero vector xxx such that Ax=λxAx=\lambda xAx=λx for some scalar λ\lambdaλ. A scalar λ\lambdaλ is called an eigenvalue of AAA if there is a nontrivial solution xxx of Ax=λxAx=\lambda xAx=λx; such xxx is called an eigenvector corresponding to λ\lambdaλ

Note that the eigenvector must be nonzero, but eigenvalue can be zero.

But if we want to know whether λ\lambdaλ is a eigenvalue of AAA, there is a method that:

Ax=λx⇒(A−λI)x=0Ax=\lambda x \Rightarrow (A-\lambda I)x=0 Ax=λx⇒(A−λI)x=0

has a nontrivial solution

The solution of all (A−λI)x=0(A-\lambda I)x=0(A−λI)x=0 is the null space of A−λIA-\lambda IA−λI, so this is a subset of Rn\R^nRn and it is called the eigenspace of AAA corresponding to λ\lambdaλ. The eigenspace consists of the zero vector and all the eigenvectors corresponding to λ\lambdaλ.

Theorem 1

The eigenvalues of a triangular matrix are the entries on its main diagonal.

Proof:

If (A−λI)x=0(A-\lambda I)x=0(A−λI)x=0 has a nontrivial solution, then it has at least one free variables.

0 is an eigenvalue of AAA iff AAA is not invertible.

Section 5.2 The Characteristic Equation

Find the eigenvalues of a matrix AAA.

It is equivalent to find all the scalars λ\lambdaλ that A−IλA-I\lambdaA−Iλ is not invertible, that det⁡(A−Iλ)=0\det(A-I\lambda) =0det(A−Iλ)=0

Theorem 3 Properties of Determinants

Let AAA and BBB be n×nn\times nn×n matrix.

  1. AAA is invertible iff det⁡A≠0\det A \neq 0detA=0

  2. det⁡AB=(det⁡A)(det⁡B)\det AB=(\det A)(\det B)detAB=(detA)(detB)

  3. det⁡AT=det⁡A\det A^T=\det AdetAT=detA

  4. If AAA is triangular, then det⁡A\det AdetA is the product of the entries on the main diagonal of AAA.

  5. A row replacement operation on AAA does not change the determinant.

    A row interchange changes the sign of the determinant.

    A row scaling also scales the determinant by the same scalar factor.

Theorem The Invertible Matrix Theorem (continued)

  1. The number 0 is not an eigenvalue of AAA

The Characteristic Equation

A scalar λ\lambdaλ is an eigenvalue of an n×nn\times nn×n matrix AAA iff λ\lambdaλ satisfies the characteristic equation

det⁡(A−λI)=0\det(A-\lambda I ) = 0 det(A−λI)=0

Use λ\lambdaλ as a parameter, and then change $A-\lambda I $ into the triangular form.

It can be shown that if AAA is an n×nn\times nn×n matrix, then det⁡(A−λI)\det (A-\lambda I )det(A−λI) is a polynomial of degree nnn called the characteristic polynomial of AAA

The (algebraic) multiplicity of an eigenvalue λ\lambdaλ is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic equation.

Similarity

If AAA and BBB are n×nn\times nn×n matrices, then AAA is similar to BBB if there is an invertible matrix PPP such that P−1AP=BP^{-1}AP=BP−1AP=B, or, equivalently, A=PBP−1A=PBP^{-1}A=PBP−1.

Changing AAA into P−1APP^{-1}APP−1AP is called a similarity transformation.

Theorem 4

If n×nn\times nn×n matrices AAA and BBB are similar, then they have the same characteristic polynomial and hence the same eigenvalues (with the same multiplicities).

  1. Although two matrices have the same eigenvalues, they may not be similar.
  2. Similarity is not the same as row equivalence.

Section 5.3 Diagonalization

Theorem 5 The Diagonalization Theorem

An n×nn\times nn×n matrix AAA is diagonalizable iff AAA has nnn linearly independent eigenvectors.

In fact, A=PDP−1A=PDP^{-1}A=PDP−1, with DDD a diagonal matrix, iff the columns of PPP are linearly independent eigenvectors of AAA. In this case, the diagonal entries of DDD are eigenvalues of AAA that correspond, respectively, to the eigenvectors in PPP.

In other words, AAA is diagonalizable iff there are enough eigenvectors to form a basis of Rn\R^nRn. We call such a basis an eigenvector basis of Rn\R^nRn

Diagonalizing Matrices

  1. Find the eigenvalues of AAA
  2. Find the linearly independent eigenvectors of AAA
  3. Construct PPP from the vectors in step 2
  4. Construct DDD from the corresponding eigenvalues

Theorem 6

An n×nn\times nn×n matrix with nnn distinct eigenvalues is diagonalizable.

Matrices Whose Eigenvalues Are Not Distinct

Theorem 7

Let AAA be an n×nn\times nn×n matrix whose distinct eigenvalues are λ1,…,λp\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_pλ1​,…,λp​.

  1. For 1≤k≤p1\le k\le p1≤k≤p, the dimension of the eigenspace for λk\lambda _kλk​ is less than or equal to ghe multiplicity of the eigenvalue λk\lambda_kλk​.
  2. The matrix AAA is diagonalizable iff the sum of the dimensions of the eigenspaces equals nnn, and this happens iff
    1. the characteristic polynomial factors completely into linear factors
    2. the dimension of the eigenspace for each λk\lambda_kλk​ equals the multiplicity of λk\lambda_kλk​
  3. If AAA is diagonalizable and BkB_kBk​ is a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to λk\lambda_kλk​ for each kkk, then the total collection of vectors in sets B1,…,BpB_1,\dots,B_pB1​,…,Bp​ forms an eigenvectors basis for Rn\R^nRn.
